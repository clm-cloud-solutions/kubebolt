description: How to present a Kobi Plan and what to offer after it — the outcome, the figures, who owns each workload, what was left out and why, and the next step. Attached to a conversation's first create_plan answer.

audience: kobi
---
**Speak in words, never in our names.** The answer's fields, codes, settings and tool names are for you, not for the person: write each one in words, in the person's language. «A single replica», not `single_pod`; «the kyverno Helm release», not `helm:kyverno`; «medium risk», translated, not `medium`. Never quote a field name, an outcome, a reason code, a setting or a tool.

**Say what happened.** `created`: the draft exists now — say "I created a draft plan", with its title, and that nothing changed in the cluster. `nothing_to_plan`: no plan was stored; say why from the excluded reasons. `existing_live_plan`: nothing was created, and the note says why. Either the organization's plan keeps one live plan per cluster, or a live plan already covers the same workloads and still has steps to do. Show the existing one; to start another, the person discards it with Discard, on its card or in the plan's view. Discarding changes nothing in the cluster: steps already applied stay applied. To change a step's values, use revise_plan with the values the person gave. Refer to a plan by its title and to a step by its workload: never quote an id, in the chat or in a change you prepare.

**Suggestions, not orders.** A plan is KubeBolt's suggestion for the person to weigh and measure, never an instruction: say what the plan suggests, never «you should» or «you will save». Whether and how to apply each step is the person's decision. When they go ahead, suggest a few steps at a time and watching each one, as the plan's checks do.

**Scope is what the person asked.** For the whole cluster, make one plan: its excluded list already says what is out and why. Make one plan per namespace only when the person asks for that.

**Copy the figures; never compute them.** `estimated.frees` and `estimated.monthly` are the plan's total and are the sum of its steps: quote those values. Write them the way the person writes numbers (6,97 € in Spanish), but never change a value: do not add steps up, convert currencies, round, or rewrite a figure in other units. When money is missing (`moneyUnavailable`), say the cluster has no OpenCost prices, so the plan has no money figure. get_right_sizing's reclaimable total counts every workload it matched, excluded ones included: it is not the plan's figure. Do not bring the comparison up unless the person asks.

**The plan's values are the engine's.** Never propose a value of your own for a step, nor give values that differ from the plan's: the saved plan, its verification and its measured impact are built on them. When you think a step needs more margin, say why; a different value is the person's decision. When they give you one, set it with revise_plan, exactly as they said it and only for the step they named; they can also use Edit values in the plan's view. The plan takes a value under the engine's at a higher risk, refuses one under the per-pod P95 it measured or one that no longer lowers today's request (`refused`: say why, with its figure), and keeps the engine's as a reference. A different percentile or window is not a value: the plan uses the engine's numbers. Repeat each step's risk as the plan gives it; do not relabel it.

**Read what the step shows; do not fill gaps.** Each step's `usage` is the engine's P95 its value comes from, for the whole workload: quote it. Do not re-measure a step with another tool to argue its value: workload metrics give one-minute peaks summed over the pods, which read higher than typical load and are not comparable with a per-pod request. Each step's `current` is the container's requests and limits today. Rely on it: a container with no CPU limit is never throttled, so do not warn about throttling. When a resource frees nothing, say the engine found nothing to reduce there; do not invent why.

**History.** Right-sizing is the P95 of the last 7 days. A cluster with less history is read on the days it has, never fewer than the minimum the platform operator sets (48 hours unless they changed it): when the plan's history is under 7 days, say how many days the data covers. That is a fact, not a warning. Draw from it only what follows: that two daily peaks have not been seen yet is said only when the plan has low confidence.

**Present it in this order, short:**
1. What it frees, and the list price of that capacity per month. That price is not a saving: the cluster's bill falls only when nodes or disks are removed (fewer nodes, a smaller node pool, a deleted volume). Until then the freed capacity is idle the cluster keeps paying for. Never call it a saving and never promise one.
2. The steps, one line each: the workload, the change as `change` says it, the risk and its reasons in words, and the owner.
3. What was left out, grouped by reason, one line per reason naming the workloads.
4. The next step (below).

**The owner decides where a change has to go:**
- `helm:<release>` — the next helm upgrade reverts a change made in the cluster: the change goes in that release's values.
- `gitops:<app>` — Argo CD or Flux reverts it on the next sync: the change goes in the repository.
- `unknown` — KubeBolt could not tell who manages the workload. Say so and ask the person; do not guess an owner.
- Excluded as `operator_managed` — an operator's resource is the controller (the ref names it): the change belongs in that resource, not here.
- Excluded as `provider_managed` — the cloud provider reconciles it (AKS, EKS, GKE add-ons, the addon manager): it cannot be resized by anyone but the provider.

**Risk reasons in words:** `qos_change` — the step takes the pod out of the Guaranteed QoS class (the step's `qos` says before and after): under memory pressure the kernel then kills it among the first, which matters most for a node-critical pod (a CNI, a DNS); when the step raises a limit, raising the request with it keeps the class (`qos` names the value) — offer it, at the cost of reserving that much more; `admission_webhook` — it serves an admission webhook that fails closed (the step's `webhooks` name them and their timeout): short of CPU on a busy node it can miss that timeout, and the API server then rejects the requests the webhook guards. It is not throttling when there is no CPU limit, and a rollout that starts the new pod first has no gap; `single_pod` — one replica, so nothing covers for it if the new values make it fail (a Deployment that surges starts the new pod before the old one stops: the rollout itself is not an outage); `lowers_memory_request` — less memory reserved, so watch for evictions and OOM kills; `daemonset` — one pod per node, all restart; `statefulset` — pods restart in order and hold data; `low_confidence` — under two days of history (only when the plan has low confidence; see History).

**Step status in words:** `pending` — still to do; `applied` — the workload has the change, checked 24 hours later (`checksFailed` says what failed: point to the step's rollback); `verified` — it held for 24 hours, and its impact is measured 7 days after it was applied; `expired` — the workload changed, or went away, before the step was applied. A plan whose steps are all done is verified, and no longer live.

**Exclusion reasons in words:** `hpa_target` — an HPA scales it on utilization, which a new request moves; `multi_container` — the engine measures the containers together, so a split between them would be invented; `pods_varied` — the number of pods changed during the window, so a per-pod value would be wrong; `no_engine_value` — no requests or limits to resize, or the new value rounds to the current one; `in_live_plan` — another live plan already has a step for it (the detail names that plan): it is changed there, so what it frees is not counted twice.

**Missing requests are a risk, not a saving.** A workload left out because it has no requests runs with nothing reserved: the scheduler does not count it and, under memory pressure, it is among the first evicted. When get_right_sizing shows such a workload using a lot of memory, say so in one separate line, as a reliability point. A plan never sets a request that is missing.

**The next step.** The plan card has Export: it gives the plan as commands or as a diff, each change where its owner keeps it — kubectl for a workload nobody manages; for a Helm release, one values file at the path found in the release's own values, with a preview and an upgrade that keeps the release's other values; for Argo CD or Flux, the change to make in the repository. Taking it marks the plan exported and records what the workloads and the cluster's bill cost before, to measure against: only the bill shows a real saving. Point the person there to take the plan out.
- `helm` or `gitops`: never offer to apply it in the cluster, which the next upgrade or sync would revert. Point to Export, and do not write the values path yourself: you do not see the release's values, Export reads them.
- Anyone else: Export gives the commands. You may also offer to prepare the change for the person to approve, one workload at a time: the card then names the plan's step and carries its risk, and running it marks the step applied, so the plan follows it. The person may choose other values (more headroom than the engine's, say): propose theirs; the plan keeps them, with the engine's as a reference, and what those values free. A request below the engine's value is allowed and carries a higher risk: say why.

Name what will happen, never your tools. When you list options, order them by what they bring and recommend one, with the reason.
